# Cherry Blossom Bloom Dynamics: Project Summary

## Why We Chose This Problem

Cherry blossom timing gives us a visible biological record of climate. A bloom date is easy to understand, but it also carries information about temperature, geography and long-term environmental change. We chose this project to study two related questions:

1. How has the timing of cherry blossom flowering changed through time?
2. Can a simple dynamical or temperature-based equation explain that change?

The project combines Kyoto's unusually long historical record with the shorter but geographically broad Japanese Meteorological Agency (JMA) station dataset. Kyoto gives the long historical perspective. The stations show whether the pattern is local or national.

The detailed workflow and hypothesis history are maintained in [research_log.md](research_log.md). Add a dated entry there whenever a substantial new analysis changes the interpretation, data decisions or remaining questions.

The concise continuation guide for a new AI session is [next_chat_handoff.md](next_chat_handoff.md). It lists the adopted equation, exact outputs, commands and remaining work.

The expansion plan for the next stage is documented in [research_architecture_plan.md](research_architecture_plan.md). It defines the canonical data layers, validation gates, degree-day and chilling experiments, joint duration model and final deliverables.

The supplied nonlinear-dynamics reference paper is summarized in [reference_paper_notes.md](reference_paper_notes.md). It motivates state variables, moving equilibria, relaxation and regime-change tests, but it does not justify claiming a tipping point in our bloom data.

## What Has Been Done

### 1. Kyoto historical case study

The Aono Kyoto series, covering approximately 812 AD to the present, was cleaned and analysed separately from the modern station data. The important correction was to avoid using reconstructed pre-instrumental temperatures as if they were independent observations. Those temperatures were partly derived from bloom dates, so the original very high $R^2$ was circular.

The final Kyoto results use the 118 years with observed March temperatures. The main results are stored in [kyoto_results.txt](../data/kyoto_results.txt) and are produced by [kyoto_master_analysis.py](../scripts/kyoto_master_analysis.py).

- Bloom timing changes by **−2.99 days per degree Celsius**; 95% CI **[−3.39, −2.60]**.
- The observed-temperature regression has $R^2 = 0.6623$.
- The Durbin-Watson statistic is **2.03**, with no obvious autocorrelation signal.
- The post-1850 trend is **−7.53 days per century**; 95% CI **[−9.16, −5.89]**.
- The pre-1850 trend is small and later: **+0.31 days per century**; 95% CI **[+0.12, +0.50]**.
- The post-1950 trend is **−7.93 days per century**, with an overlapping confidence interval. There is no strong evidence here that the recent trend accelerated.
- The post-1990 residual check does not show a significant shift: mean residual **−0.28 days**, $p = 0.63$.
- The temperature-by-post-1950 interaction is not significant: $p = 0.22$.

The main Kyoto figures are [step1_doy_vs_year.png](../plots/step1_doy_vs_year.png), [doy_trends_split.png](../plots/doy_trends_split.png), [residuals_vs_year_observed.png](../plots/residuals_vs_year_observed.png), and the observed-only temperature figure [kyoto_observed_doy_vs_temperature.png](../plots/kyoto_observed_doy_vs_temperature.png). The histogram [step1_doy_distribution.png](../plots/step1_doy_distribution.png) shows the overall distribution of Kyoto full-bloom dates.

### 2. JMA station trends

The JMA dataset contains first-bloom and full-bloom dates for 102 stations. Dates were reshaped from wide to long format. Bloom dates use a leap-year-normalized day of year, while duration uses the actual datetime difference. This matters for Taiwan cherry stations whose season crosses the New Year.

The main station analysis excludes Taiwan cherry, Kurile cherry and Kutchan, whose observed species changed. The cleaned histogram contains **90 stations**: 80 stations labelled Not stated and 10 Sargent cherry stations. The label Not stated is treated as Yoshino for the main comparison, but this remains an assumption that should be confirmed from the dataset documentation.

The cleaned station trend figure is [histogram_full_trends.png](../plots/histogram_full_trends.png), generated by [jma_trend_histogram.py](../scripts/jma_trend_histogram.py).

The 1961-2010 common-window analysis retains 73 Not stated stations and 8 Sargent stations, requiring at least 40 valid years. In that window:

- **98.6%** of Not stated stations have a negative full-bloom trend.
- **87.5%** of Sargent stations have a negative full-bloom trend.
- Not stated full-bloom trend median: **−1.27 days/decade**, IQR **[−1.58, −0.96]**.
- Sargent full-bloom trend median: **−1.09 days/decade**, IQR **[−1.44, −0.81]**.
- Not stated duration trend median: **+0.30 days/decade**.
- Sargent duration trend median: **−0.21 days/decade**.

The common-window results come from [jma_deep_checks.py](../scripts/jma_deep_checks.py). The older [station_trends.csv](../data/JMA-kaggle_dataest/station_trends.csv) is legacy output and includes species groups that should not be used for the final histogram.

The window-sensitivity check in [jma_window_robustness.py](../scripts/jma_window_robustness.py) shows that the earlier full-bloom result is stable. For Not stated stations, 98.6%, 98.0% and 97.9% of stations have negative full-bloom trends in the 1961-2010, 1971-2020 and 1976-2025 windows, respectively. The median trend becomes more negative from -1.27 to -1.36 to -1.62 days/decade. The duration median is less stable, changing from +0.30 to +0.24 to +0.10 days/decade. This supports a robust national advance in bloom timing, but a more window-sensitive duration pattern.

### 3. Duration and regional structure

The national duration median hides an important geographic pattern. Some northern stations show shortening, while many southern and central stations show lengthening. The station names suggested this pattern first, and the coordinate analysis now supports it.

Coordinates were replaced using the official JMA AMeDAS station master, not general geocoding. The reproducible updater is [update_jma_coordinates.py](../scripts/update_jma_coordinates.py), and the resulting table is [station_coords_template.csv](../data/JMA-kaggle_dataest/station_coords_template.csv).

For 73 Yoshino-group stations in the common window:

- Mean full-bloom date becomes later by **3.541 days per degree latitude**; $R^2 = 0.873$.
- The full-bloom trend becomes more negative with latitude: **−0.062 days/decade per degree**; $R^2 = 0.115$.
- The duration trend becomes more negative with latitude: **−0.104 days/decade per degree**, 95% CI **[−0.134, −0.073]**, $R^2 = 0.395$.

The result means latitude is strongly related to the average bloom date and is also related to the direction of duration change. It does not by itself prove that latitude causes the trend. Latitude may represent several linked factors, including winter temperature, chilling conditions, elevation, coastal influence and station history. The results and figure are produced by [jma_latitude_analysis.py](../scripts/jma_latitude_analysis.py) and [latitude_regressions.png](../plots/latitude_regressions.png).

Aikawa was checked separately and is nearly flat, about **−0.02 days/decade**. It is useful as a reminder that the national pattern is not identical at every station. Yonaguni Island has only **16 observations**, so its [sparse_trend.png](../plots/sparse_trend.png) should be treated as a low-power illustration rather than strong evidence. The Aikawa figure is [aikawa_trend.png](../plots/aikawa_trend.png).

### 4. Temperature sensitivity across stations

The official JMA daily observations have now been collected for all 20 planned stations from 1953-2024. The streaming downloader is [fetch_jma_daily_streaming.py](../scripts/fetch_jma_daily_streaming.py), the daily data are in [jma_daily_observed_streaming.csv](../data/jma_daily_observed_streaming.csv), and the canonical monthly join is [station_year_weather_jma.csv](../data/station_year_weather_jma.csv). The dataset contains 85,216 daily rows and 1,355 eligible phenology-weather station-years. Nara has no JMA daily February/March records for 1953; this is documented as a source gap.

The earlier Open-Meteo comparison remains available in [station_year_weather.csv](../data/station_year_weather.csv), but the JMA-observed dataset is now preferred for physical and temperature models. The aggregation and validation script is [build_jma_weather_table.py](../scripts/build_jma_weather_table.py).

The station analysis compares full-bloom date with March temperature and duration with the February-March mean temperature. The figure is [temperature_sensitivity.png](../plots/temperature_sensitivity.png), generated by [jma_temperature_sensitivity.py](../scripts/jma_temperature_sensitivity.py).

The preliminary pattern is consistent with the duration results:

- Kyoto duration sensitivity: **+1.41 days per degree Celsius**, $R^2 = 0.40$.
- Fukuoka: **+1.55 days per degree Celsius**.
- Kagoshima: **+1.06 days per degree Celsius**.
- Osaka: **+1.20 days per degree Celsius**.
- Aomori: **−0.14 days per degree Celsius**, $R^2 = 0.01$.
- Asahikawa: **−0.14 days per degree Celsius**, $R^2 = 0.02$.
- Yamagata: **−0.07 days per degree Celsius**, approximately no relationship.

With the expanded 19-station set, 13 duration-temperature slopes are positive and 6 are negative. A simple exploratory regression of the 19 station-level slopes against latitude gives a negative slope and $R^2 \approx 0.715$. This is consistent with the regional hypothesis, but it is not a final independent-station significance test because nearby stations share weather and the temperature source is external to the JMA phenology source.

This is evidence for a regional temperature-duration relationship, not a final causal test. The preferred weather series are now JMA daily observations, although neighbouring stations remain spatially correlated.

The focused outputs are [station_temperature_sensitivity.csv](../data/station_temperature_sensitivity.csv) and [station_duration_slope_latitude.csv](../data/station_duration_slope_latitude.csv), generated by [station_physical_analysis.py](../scripts/station_physical_analysis.py). The 19-station latitude-duration coefficient is **−0.1037 days/decade per degree**, $R^2 = 0.3947$.

Using JMA observations instead of Open-Meteo changes the coefficients but preserves the broad regional pattern: JMA duration slopes are Kyoto **+1.18**, Fukuoka **+1.37**, Kagoshima **+1.10**, Aomori **−0.21**, Akita **−0.10** and Wakkanai **−0.28** days per degree Celsius. The JMA table is [station_temperature_sensitivity_jma.csv](../data/station_temperature_sensitivity_jma.csv).

The separate 20-station regression of duration-temperature slope against latitude is stored in [station_duration_temperature_slope_latitude_jma.csv](../data/station_duration_temperature_slope_latitude_jma.csv). Its slope is **−0.1175 days/decade per latitude degree**, 95% CI **[−0.1527, −0.0823]**, $R^2 = 0.7318$. This is distinct from the 73-station regression of duration trend against latitude, which has slope **−0.1037** and $R^2 = 0.3947$.

### 5. Physical equation attempt

A simple 400-degree thermal-sum rule was tested for Kyoto using daily temperatures. Positive daily temperatures were accumulated from February until the total reached 400 degree-days. A linear Feb-March temperature model was used as a comparison.

The figure is [step4_physical_equation.png](../plots/step4_physical_equation.png), generated by [step4_physical_equation.py](../scripts/step4_physical_equation.py).

- 400-degree rule RMSE: **4.84 days**.
- Linear Feb-March model RMSE: **3.24 days** on the fitted comparison data.

The thermal-sum model is scientifically meaningful because it represents accumulated heat rather than only a seasonal average. However, the current implementation is a first baseline, not a validated physical model. The starting date, threshold temperature, degree-day threshold and chilling requirement all need calibration. The comparison also needs a clearly defined out-of-sample evaluation before claiming predictive superiority.

Using the same chronological split for both models, with training years $\leq 1990$ and test years $>1990$, the fixed degree-day model has RMSE **4.6820 days in-sample** and **3.6015 days out-of-sample**. The linear Feb-March model has **3.1264 days in-sample** and **4.2782 days out-of-sample**. These temperatures are Open-Meteo archive data, not JMA observed temperatures.

With official JMA daily Kyoto observations, the fixed model has **9.3941 days in-sample** and **7.2938 days out-of-sample** RMSE, compared with **2.3138** and **4.0473 days** for the matched linear model. This fixed 400-degree rule is therefore not an accurate final physical model. The JMA comparison uses 37 paired Kyoto years and is recorded in [kyoto_jma_degree_day_results.csv](../data/kyoto_jma_degree_day_results.csv).

The simplest adopted physical/statistical model is therefore the temperature-response equation:

$$
D = a - bT_{March}
$$

For the 72-year Kyoto JMA station series, the fitted equation is:

$$
D_{Kyoto} = 119.55 - 2.878T_{March}
$$

with $R^2 = 0.720$. This is an empirical temperature-response model, not a complete plant-process equation. The degree-day model remains a transparent but rejected fixed-parameter mechanism.

The station restoring-force experiment is recorded in [station_restoring_force.csv](../data/station_restoring_force.csv) and [station_phase_space_selected.png](../plots/station_phase_space_selected.png). Fits of $d(DOY)/dt = -k DOY + c$ for Wakkanai, Kagoshima, Matsumoto and Kyoto had $R^2$ values below **0.003**, so the raw annual autonomous restoring-force model is treated as diagnostic rather than adopted as the main station model.

The first temperature-forced moving-equilibrium comparison is in [station_moving_equilibrium.csv](../data/station_moving_equilibrium.csv) and [station_moving_equilibrium.png](../plots/station_moving_equilibrium.png). It uses consecutive years, training through 1990 and testing after 1990. All 19 stations fit. The median held-out RMSE is **4.4132 days**, compared with **4.5232 days** for the same-split station-specific temperature baseline, with improvement at 10 of 19 stations. Eighteen of 19 relaxation confidence intervals include zero, so this remains an exploratory dynamics result, not evidence of tipping or a final superior model.

With JMA monthly observations, the moving-equilibrium model has median held-out RMSE **4.3176 days**, compared with **4.0455 days** for the baseline, and improves at only 9 of 19 stations. All 19 relaxation confidence intervals include zero. The JMA rerun is in [station_moving_equilibrium_jma.csv](../data/station_moving_equilibrium_jma.csv).

### Limit-cycle follow-up

A focused audit of Kyoto, Wakkanai, Kagoshima and Osaka compared first and full bloom separately. Across the four stations, smooth phase-space paths did not consistently close: median endpoint-distance/path-length ratios were **0.418** for the `s/n=25` splines and **0.328** for 7-year Gaussian-kernel paths. Candidate periods after three detrending choices explained about **10--18%** of residual variance; no autocorrelation-aware significance test was performed. In matched chronological holdouts, a training-selected periodic term beat the temperature-only baseline at **1 of 8** station/stage pairs. These findings do not establish a limit cycle or periodic climate response.

The reproducible follow-up outputs are [advanced dynamics station audit](../data/advanced_dynamics_station_audit.csv), [smoothing sensitivity](../data/phase_space_smoothing_sensitivity.csv), [period screen](../data/phase_space_oscillation_screen.csv), [recurrence audit](../data/phase_space_recurrence_audit.csv), and [held-out model scores](../data/recurrence_model_holdout_scores.csv). Details and plot placeholders are in the [dynamical report](dynamical_analysis_report_v2.tex); decisions are recorded in [research log H27--H30](research_log.md).

## What the Work Means

The strongest conclusion is that cherry blossom timing has advanced across Japan, and Kyoto's long record shows that the modern change is especially clear after the nineteenth century. The observed-temperature Kyoto analysis supports a strong relationship between warmer March conditions and earlier flowering.

The station results add an important detail. Bloom dates are nationally consistent, but bloom duration is not. A near-zero national median does not mean duration is unchanged everywhere. Opposing regional changes can cancel in a national summary. The latitude and temperature analyses suggest that warmer southern and central regions may experience a longer interval between first and full bloom, while northern stations more often show compression.

The work is significant because it connects three scales of evidence:

- Kyoto gives a long historical climate-sensitive record.
- The JMA stations show spatial consistency across Japan.
- The temperature and physical-model analyses begin to connect the observed dates to mechanisms.

The study should still be presented as observational evidence. It does not establish that temperature is the only cause, and the station trends are not 102 independent experiments because nearby stations share weather and local climate.

## What Is Left To Do

2. **Use the adopted simple equation.** Present $D_{Kyoto} = 119.55 - 2.878T_{March}$ as the empirical physical-response model, with its uncertainty and limitations.
3. **Retain the regional duration result cautiously.** The trend and temperature-slope latitude analyses are separate exploratory associations, not a causal or spatially independent model.
4. **Complete the Taiwan cherry analysis separately.** Season-relative dates from January 1 are appropriate, but Taiwan cherry should not be pooled with Yoshino or Sargent cherry.
5. **Keep the degree-day result as a rejected baseline.** Do not expand into grid-search or chilling models within this project unless a future project is specifically devoted to biological calibration.
6. **Check source overlap.** Confirm whether recent Aono Kyoto observations overlap the JMA Kyoto station series and avoid treating overlapping observations as independent.
7. **Confirm species labels.** Verify what Not stated means in the Kaggle/JMA source description rather than relying on the Yoshino assumption.
8. **Keep nonlinear-dynamics claims limited.** The restoring-force and moving-equilibrium models remain exploratory; the four-station loop follow-up did not validate a closed cycle or show a held-out advantage from a periodic term. No tipping or bifurcation was demonstrated.
9. **Create one final pipeline.** Combine the cleaned Kyoto, station, coordinate, JMA weather and plotting steps into a documented reproducible workflow. Keep exploratory scripts clearly marked as legacy.
10. **Prepare the final report.** Use the observed-only Kyoto temperature figure, the cleaned station histogram, the latitude figure and the temperature-duration figure. Do not use the original mixed reconstructed/observed temperature plots.

## Suggested Overall Conclusion

The evidence supports a clear advance in cherry blossom timing in Japan, strongly linked to warmer spring temperatures in the observed Kyoto era. The geographic analysis suggests that bloom duration responds differently across Japan, with a likely north-south structure that is hidden by national averages. The adopted simple physical-response model is $D_{Kyoto} = 119.55 - 2.878T_{March}$ using official JMA observations. Degree-day and moving-equilibrium models were tested as exploratory comparisons but were not adopted as final equations. The next stage is final verification and presentation, not expansion into a larger biological or tipping-point model.
