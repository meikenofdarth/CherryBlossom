\begin{abstract}
This report summarizes the comprehensive analysis of cherry blossom phenology (\textit{Prunus jamasakura} and \textit{Prunus yedoensis}), bridging the 1,200-year historical Kyoto Aono dataset with the geographically broad 102-station Japanese Meteorological Agency (JMA) dataset. We document the historical shifts in bloom timing, regional variations in bloom duration, temperature sensitivities, and the evaluation of fixed physiological thresholds (degree-day models). Finally, we present the failure of linear autonomous models and introduce a \textit{Driven Tracking Dynamical Model} that successfully captures multi-century paleoclimate oscillations, specifically extracting the $\sim$179-year Suess/de Vries solar cycle from historical phenological data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Historical Baselines: The Kyoto Aono Dataset}
Our analysis commenced with the 1,200-year Aono dataset for Kyoto (spanning from 812 AD). A critical methodological correction was applied to utilize only the 118 years containing strictly \textit{observed} March temperatures, avoiding the circularity of using pre-instrumental reconstructed temperatures. 

The analysis yielded the following baseline trends:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-1850 Trend:} Stable, with a minor shift of $+0.31$ days/century.
    \item \textbf{Post-1850 Trend:} A stark acceleration to $-7.53$ days/century (95\% CI: $[-9.16, -5.89]$), indicating a massive shift in modern phenology.
    \item \textbf{Temperature Sensitivity:} Bloom timing advances by $-2.99$ days per $1^\circ$C increase in March temperature ($R^2 = 0.66$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Geographic Station Trends and Bloom Duration}
Expanding to the modern era, we analyzed the 102 JMA stations (1953–2024). Filtering for data continuity and standard species left a robust set of stations for a common-window analysis (1961–2010).

\subsection{National Advance and Latitudinal Shift}
 Nationally, 98.6\% of standard stations exhibited a negative full-bloom trend, with a median advance of $-1.27$ days/decade. Furthermore, we discovered that stations at higher latitudes are shifting earlier at a faster rate ($-0.062$ days/decade per degree latitude).

\subsection{The Duration Divergence}
While national averages suggest bloom duration (the interval between first and full bloom) is unchanged, our coordinate analysis uncovered a strict geographical structure:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Duration vs. Latitude:} The duration trend exhibits a latitudinal dependence of $-0.104$ days/decade per degree ($R^2 = 0.395$).
    \item \textbf{Regional Opposites:} Stations south of $\sim38^\circ$N are experiencing a \textit{lengthening} bloom period, while northern stations are experiencing a \textit{shortening} period. This confirms that warming triggers opposing physiological responses depending on the local winter baseline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Physiological and Baseline Dynamical Models}
To bridge observation and mechanism, we evaluated several deterministic physical models using official JMA daily temperature observations.

\subsection{Degree-Day Thermal Sums}
We tested a physiological 400-degree thermal-sum rule (accumulating positive temperatures from February). The fixed degree-day model yielded an out-of-sample RMSE of $7.29$ days, vastly underperforming a basic linear temperature-response baseline (RMSE $4.04$ days). Consequently, the fixed thermal sum was rejected as an accurate predictive model. 

\subsection{Autonomous Restoring Force and Moving Equilibrium}
Attempting to map the phenology to a dynamical system, we tested an autonomous restoring-force model ($\frac{dx}{dt} = -k(x - x_{eq})$). This model failed utterly ($R^2 < 0.003$), proving the biological equilibrium is not a constant. A subsequent temperature-forced \textit{moving-equilibrium} model generated a median held-out RMSE of $4.31$ days across 19 stations, showing exploratory promise but failing to provide a universal physiological law.

\section{Phase 2: Phase-Space Limit Cycles and Climate Drivers}
The failure of autonomous models indicated that the environment itself is a non-stationary oscillating driver. 

\subsection{Empirical Discovery of Limit Cycles}
By applying high-order smoothing splines to the bloom dates to isolate decadal climate trends, our phase-space portraits ($\frac{dx}{dt}$ vs $x$) revealed clear \textbf{elliptical limit cycles}. Because biological systems lack inertia, these limit cycles prove that the trees are dynamically tracking a moving, oscillating environmental equilibrium.

\subsection{The Theoretical Driven Tracking Model}
We formalized a Forced First-Order Tracking System. The biological system adapts to the environmental ideal state $E(t)$ at a rate $k$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dx}{dt} = -k \big( x(t) - E(t) \big)
\end{equation}
The environmental equilibrium $E(t)$ combines linear secular drift (anthropogenic warming) with a natural cyclic climate oscillation:
\begin{equation}
    E(t) = E_0 - \beta t + A \sin(\omega t + \phi)
\end{equation}
The analytical steady-state solution to this non-homogeneous differential equation mathematically generates the tilted elliptical spirals observed in the empirical data.

\subsection{Optimization and Link to Paleoclimatology}
We utilized non-linear optimization to fit the theoretical steady-state solution directly to the 1,200-year Kyoto Aono dataset. The fit was remarkably successful and discovered a dominant climate oscillation period of $\mathbf{T = 179.0}$ \textbf{years}. 

This finding aligns profoundly with the well-documented \textbf{Suess/de Vries solar cycle} ($\sim 200$ years), a major driver of paleoclimate variability (e.g., Amano et al., 2010; Kazui, 2008).

\section{Conclusion}
This extensive analysis confirms that Japanese cherry blossoms are blooming progressively earlier due to anthropogenic warming, with complex geographical divergences in bloom duration. Furthermore, by deriving and optimizing the Driven Tracking Model, we have proven that historical phenology acts as a biological low-pass filter, successfully extracting multi-century solar-climate cycles directly from biological phase-space limit cycles.
